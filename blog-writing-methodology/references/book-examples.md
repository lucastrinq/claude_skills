# Writing Attractively — Content That Converts Principles

Format-agnostic craft principles from Devin Reed's *Content That Converts*, underlying the
destination skeletons and workflow in the main skill. Pull from here when a concrete framework,
attribution, or worked example would strengthen a recommendation — not needed for every draft.

### The value equation: insightful + relevant + actionable
- **Insightful** — surprising enough that a reader's eyebrows actually move.
- **Relevant** — tied to what this specific persona cares about right now, anchored to their problem.
- **Actionable** — paired with a next step: an example, a step-by-step, a copy-paste template, or a checklist. Insight without a next step reads as trivia.

The test throughout: what does the reader get out of this? Value happens before the CTA, not after.

### A clear point of view beats a neutral recap
A piece that takes an actual stance — even mildly contrarian — travels further and builds a more
qualified following than one that objectively summarizes a topic. Worth checking on
educational/thought-leadership pieces specifically: is there a real point of view here, or just
a summary?

### Two opener shapes
A **benefit-led** opener states the payoff up front. A **curiosity-led** opener withholds it
briefly to create an information gap. Curiosity openers only work if the gap resolves quickly in
the piece — an unresolved one reads as clickbait.

### The rule of three
When listing examples, symptoms, or consequences, three tends to land better than two (feels
thin) or four-plus (feels padded). Worth a specific check on any bulleted list in the piece.

### Emotional first, rational second
Open with how the problem actually feels — what the reader's day looks like, what it could
become. Close with rational proof — free trial, testimonial, feature detail. Keep making the
"what's in it for you" explicit throughout both halves rather than assuming it's obvious.

### Tone, wording, and competitor positioning
Simple words, simple truths — never exaggerate, brag, or oversell. Build authority with real
specifics (years, customers, scale), not assertions ("we're the best"). Build credibility by
teaching something the reader didn't know. Use concrete, everyday examples the reader already
recognizes from their own work. Never bash a competitor by name — describe what they do or don't
do objectively, and the real risk to the reader, rather than attacking them; this is the book's
general framing, independent of AppTweak's own ASO-positioning rule (which lives in
`apptweak-writing-guidelines` and applies on top of this for the AppTweak destination).

### Data as credibility, story as the relatable wrapper
1. Start from proprietary data where possible.
2. Get into the customer's shoes — loop on "why" until it's visceral, not just functional.
3. Pick one hyper-specific scenario rather than a general claim.
4. Ask whether the data supports a broader narrative — a stat connected all the way to "here's what this means for you" is proof; a stat on its own is trivia.

### Storytelling for selling
Transport the reader from their current unwanted state to a desired future state: describe the
before, walk through the process, then paint the after — with real detail and specificity, not a
vague "things will improve." ("Gone are the days of... You'll learn to... If you use these
insights correctly, [concrete payoff].") This is the selling-focused counterpart to the
connecting-focused storytelling below — use it when the piece's job is to move someone toward an
action, not just build trust.

### Storytelling for connecting (the "content diary" approach)
A different storytelling mode, aimed at trust rather than conversion — most natural for Substack
or personal-voice pieces, but the underlying instinct (point at something real instead of
inventing an angle) applies anywhere a personal perspective is welcome.
1. **Describe your own experience plainly** — what you're stuck on, noticing, or unsure about,
   including the less flattering emotions (doubt, fear, loneliness) where genuine. Don't invent a
   take; point at something actually happening. Openers like "I noticed something," "I started
   this and...," or "this is my journey to..." signal this mode.
2. **Let the reader recognize themselves in it.** The goal isn't teaching from authority — it's
   the reader thinking "it's literally me." People increasingly follow someone going through what
   they're going through, not just someone who knows more than they do.
3. **This is not a vlog or a "film your day" post** — the personal detail is in service of a real
   idea, not a substitute for one. Craft (structure, a clear throughline, discretion about what to
   include) still matters; it just shouldn't read as polished or engineered.

**Why this matters now specifically:** AI can distribute knowledge at scale, which makes lived
experience, opinion, and personality one of the few genuinely hard-to-replicate advantages left
in content. This is the underlying case for `lucas-voice`'s Substack/personal mode — it's not
just a style preference, it's a competitive one.

### Identity and differentiation
Content built on a real, specific point of view travels further than content that could have
come from anyone. When shaping a new piece or a personal-brand angle, it's worth asking:
- What problem, experience, or perspective do you understand or notice that most people writing
  in this space don't?
- What's a commonly accepted way of operating that you actually disagree with?
- What experience do you have that conflicts with the common consensus, in a way readers could
  still relate to?

Pair this with a consistent, recognizable presentation (format, voice, visual identity) so the
point of view and the packaging reinforce each other over time, rather than each post starting
from zero.

### Social proof
Two workable approaches, and they don't have to be combined:
- **Show it** — client logos, testimonials, named results — but only when those examples actually
  resemble the reader's own situation (ICP match). A logo the reader doesn't recognize as "like
  me" does less work than one they do.
- **Name it precisely** — instead of (or alongside) showing proof, be specific enough about who
  the work is for that the right reader self-identifies: "We work with [specific role/industry]
  who [specific situation] and want to [specific outcome]." Precision reads as more credible than
  a logo wall when the audience is narrow.

### CTAs, and CTVs
Right order, most of the time: educate on the problem → grow the audience → build credibility →
only then ask for something with a real cost. **Call-to-value beats call-to-action** — pair the
ask with the outcome ("Subscribe here to get sharper at X" beats "Follow me on LinkedIn"), and
match it to the reader's actual funnel stage.

### Gating content
Default to ungated. Gating is only for a genuinely "Premium" top-of-funnel or thought-leadership
asset living on the website (an original report, a deep proprietary-data piece) — offered as a
CTA inside an otherwise-open post, not as the post itself. **Never gate content that explains the
product or the solution** — how-it-works and product-marketing content stays fully open, since
gating it just adds friction for someone already trying to become a customer.

### Two kinds of content
Most posts are **niche content** — solid, on-topic. Occasionally there's room for a **big
swing** — noticeably different, expands the niche, should feel slightly nerve-wracking to
publish. If considering one: still ask what would actually move this reader; don't benchmark it
against usual metrics, judge by upside; know the hypothesis and the risk going in; get extra
feedback before publishing.

### Titles
Specific, a clear and actionable outcome, one hyperbolic/emotional adjective. ("How to create
insanely persuasive web copy" — the what, the outcome, the charged adjective.) Vague titles or
titles missing the outcome are the two most common ways a title fails to earn the click.

### Speech structure (pitch-shaped posts)
When a post is functionally promoting something rather than purely educating: open on an
emotional, specific problem → group supporting examples/arguments in threes → get the reader
imagining their desired state → deliver the concrete how-to value → introduce what you're
promoting → circle back to the desired state, landing on the CTA/CTV. Skip this for purely
educational posts.

### Mindset (the habits behind the tactics)
None of the above compounds without the underlying habits:
- **Read consistently, write consistently.** The tactics are multipliers on a habit, not a
  substitute for one — showing up regularly is what actually builds the skill and the audience.
- **Growth = helping others.** Delivering real value to the reader isn't a trade-off against
  growth, it's the mechanism for it.
- **Hold quality, quantity, and experimentation to a real standard together** — don't let any one
  of the three quietly erode to protect the others.
- **Prioritize the reader over the dashboard.** Asking "what would help my reader most" pulls
  decisions toward quality; asking "what will move the metric" pulls them toward volume. Keep
  human-scale signals in view (a genuine reply, a repost, a follower who actually engages) rather
  than optimizing purely against an analytics dashboard.
- **Progress over perfection.** The bar is "better than last time," not flawless — chasing
  perfection is a bigger threat to consistency than any single weak piece.
