# Audience Research and A/B Test Reference

Deeper reference material for `email-newsletter-copy`: the full audience-research question set for
personalizing outreach, and Devin Reed's reported A/B test results across 10 email variables. Pull
from this file when doing a real research pass on a new audience, or when planning what to test on
a list — not needed for every send.

## Know your audience before you personalize

Research pays off in the trigger and the body both. Questions worth answering about the specific audience before writing anything:
- What do they care about, in their own terms (e.g. growing the business)?
- What are their actual day-to-day challenges (e.g. building pipeline efficiently)?
- What words do *they* use for their problems — not your internal terminology (e.g. "email campaigns" vs. "nurture sequences")?
- Why has their challenge stayed unsolved (e.g. they can't tell which part of their strategy is working)?
- What emotions attach to the problem (uncertainty, frustration, stress)?
- What motivates them beyond money (market share, expansion, recognition)?
- What do they fear, specifically (the competition, a specific objection, being wrong)?
- What quietly frustrates them more than they'd admit out loud?
- What belief do they hold that outsiders would find odd — the odder and more specific, the more it signals real audience knowledge, not a persona template?
- Are there seasonal or trend-driven shifts affecting their business right now?
- How have they been burned before by a similar promise (a bad agency engagement, a tool that underdelivered)?

Use their exact language for the problem, but skip the jargon that isn't theirs — plain words over impressive ones (e.g. in B2B: "sales rep," not "utilize"). And keep pronoun weight on them, not you: fewer "I"/"my," more "you"/"you're" — a cold email that talks about the sender first reads as self-interested before it's even made its case.

## 10 things worth A/B testing on your own list

Reed's own reported results, from testing across several B2B audiences — treat each as a hypothesis to test on your list, not a guarantee:

**For open rates:**
1. **Sender name: individual vs. company/department alias.** An individual's name consistently outperformed — a "[Company] Marketing" sender reads as "I'm being sold to" and gets deleted unread more often.
2. **Send time.** Morning sends (Reed found ~6am local strongest) edged out other times, though the effect was smaller than most people assume — don't lose sleep over exact timing.
3. **Subject line: question vs. statement.** Questions won, but only when easy to answer mentally in half a second — a confusing question underperforms a clear statement.
4. **Sentence case vs. Title Case.** Sentence case ("here's what changed") consistently beat Title Case ("Here's What Changed"), because Title Case visually signals "marketing email."

**For click-through rates:**
5. **Line breaks vs. block text.** Line breaks won every time, across audience segments — visually "lighter" copy outperforms even at identical word count.
6. **One hyperlink vs. multiple (to the same URL).** Multiple links to the same destination outperformed a single link — readers click whichever instance is nearest to where their attention already is. (This is about repeating one CTA's link, not adding multiple different CTAs.)
7. **Story vs. "hard teaching" (direct lesson format).** Stories won, and first-person stories outperformed third-person ones — people respond more to lived experience than reported experience.
8. **Shorter vs. longer copy.** Length itself didn't predict performance either way — quality and pacing mattered more than word count. (Reed's personal bias is still toward concise copy, but he's clear the data doesn't back "shorter always wins.")
9. **"Hi {First_Name}," vs. no greeting at all.** Dropping the greeting outperformed keeping it, in his tests — worth testing before assuming a greeting helps.
10. **HTML vs. plain text.** Plain text won consistently — polished HTML formatting visually signals "sales/marketing email" the same way Title Case subject lines do.
