# Email & Newsletter Copy

Tactics for the email channel specifically — newsletters, marketing sends, and cold/warm outbound (including LinkedIn InMail, which behaves like a hybrid of email and DM). Sourced and paraphrased from Devin Reed's *Content That Converts*, cross-checked against his own reported A/B tests. Treat the numbered test results below as *a* strong prior, not universal truth — his own framing is to always test against your own list before trusting someone else's results.

## Subject lines: two hook shapes

- **Benefit-led.** State the payoff plainly and let the reader decide instantly whether it's relevant to them. Straightforward, no gimmick, works well when the audience already has some trust in you or the topic is inherently high-interest.
- **Curiosity-led ("mystery").** Stay deliberately vague to spark a question the reader wants answered ("This can't be right...", "Does this work for you?"). This only works if you resolve the curiosity early in the body — an unresolved mystery hook reads as manipulative clickbait and burns trust with your list.
  - More mystery-shape examples: "Found it!", "Missing something?", "Do you see it?", "Here it is", "Is it back?", "Enough of this?", "It's happening again" — short, plain, no context given. The vagueness is the point; the subject alone shouldn't explain anything.
- Between the two: **questions generally outperform statements**, but only when the question is quick to parse. A subject line that makes the reader work to understand it kills curiosity instead of sparking it — don't confuse "vague" with "confusing."
- **Leave the reader with an unspoken question, whichever shape you pick.** A subject line's real job isn't to state something — it's to plant a question the body then answers. Test it by finishing "...which makes the reader silently ask ___?" — if nothing fills that blank, it's a statement with nowhere to go, not a hook. This applies to both shapes above: a benefit-led line can still leave a gap ("Cut your onboarding time in half" → *how?*), and a mystery line leaves one by design.

## Outreach triggers (what earns the first reply)

**Underlying principle: value over volume.** Every touch with the market (prospects, buyers,
partners) either builds your reputation with them or spends it — outbound only compounds when
each message treats the relationship as the asset, not the single reply.

A trigger gives the recipient a reason *this message, right now* — without one, an ask feels random. Two tiers, worth knowing which one fits the relationship:

- **Personalization triggers (one-to-one).** Something that applies only to this specific person — a talk they gave, a post they shared, a detail only researchable individually. Highest effort, highest reply rate. (Use judgment on how personal is too personal — referencing something like a family event can read as invasive rather than flattering.)
- **Observation triggers.** Slightly less personal, still shows research — role changes, an open headcount on their team, a public move. Good middle ground when a fully one-to-one trigger isn't feasible at scale.
- **Familiarity triggers.** Naming a real shared connection (a colleague, a mutual contact) to borrow trust immediately. Powerful specifically because so few cold messages use them — but only cite a connection that's genuinely current and relevant, not someone from years back who barely remembers the person.
- **Retire these — they no longer work:** "saw you're Head of X at Company," "congrats on the funding," "noticed you downloaded our ebook," "saw your LinkedIn profile." These were once triggers; now they're just noise everyone recognizes as automated.
- **Why triggers work at all:** if you can describe the recipient's problem better than they'd describe it themselves, they assume you have the best solution to it — trust follows precision, not friendliness. A trigger's real job is proving you did that homework before you asked for anything.
- **Use dynamic fields**, not just a hand-picked trigger — name, company, role, and similar mail-merge basics still matter as the baseline layer personalization sits on top of.
- **Where to actually find the personalization**: LinkedIn, personal websites, and other public sources — the research questions below tell you what to look for; this is where you go look for it.
- **Bridge the trigger into your offer with a connecting phrase**, so the trigger doesn't just sit there disconnected from the ask: "I'm reaching out because...", "that's why we're sharing...", "I love when you do this... and couldn't help but think...". The test behind the whole trigger + bridge move is simple: **why should my reader care?** — if the trigger doesn't lead somewhere, it's just flattery.
  - Worked example: "It looks like you're interested in leveling up your team's performance through coaching. What specifically are you working on?" — this only works because it follows either a lead-magnet download or genuine knowledge of the recipient's funnel stage; it's not a cold opener on its own.

### Know your audience before you personalize

Research pays off in the trigger and the body both. Use their exact language for the problem, but
skip the jargon that isn't theirs — plain words over impressive ones (e.g. in B2B: "sales rep,"
not "utilize"). And keep pronoun weight on them, not you: fewer "I"/"my," more "you"/"you're" — a
cold email that talks about the sender first reads as self-interested before it's even made its
case. The full research question set (what they care about, their fears, their unsolved-problem
history, and more) is in `references/research-and-ab-tests.md`.

## Structuring outbound messages (InMail / cold email)

- **Don't open with the ask.** Leading with "can we connect?" or a meeting request is the messaging equivalent of a stranger asking for 30 minutes of your time before explaining why. Open with something about the *recipient* — an observation or a shared problem — before introducing your offer.
- **Never open by apologizing or introducing yourself.** "Sorry to bother you" and "My name is X and I work for Y" both spend the reader's attention on the sender before earning it. Open with relevance instead — one of three angles:
  - **Personal interest:** something genuine and specific ("I just saw your... and loved it").
  - **Problem hook:** naming their problem before mentioning your offer.
  - **Professional hook:** a shared context ("it looks like you were at the X conference," "I saw you're also a Y alum").
- **Ask for interest before asking for time.** "Would this be useful to you?" converts better than "do you have 30 minutes?" as a first message — it's a smaller, easier yes that still moves the conversation forward.
- **Use line breaks, not block paragraphs.** Dense text signals "this is work" to the reader's brain before they've read a word of it. Short paragraphs and white space make the same message feel like less effort to read.
- **Keep it to roughly two mobile scrolls, max**, for a first outbound message.
- **An honest, slightly vulnerable closing line lands better than a polished one.** A closing that acknowledges the ask plainly (rather than a generic "looking forward to connecting!") reads as more human and less like a template.

**A dependable default structure** when starting from a blank page:
1. Hook (relevance, not self-introduction — see above)
2. The problem the reader has
3. The negative impact of that problem, stated plainly
4. A brief intro to your offer
5. The benefit of the offer (with urgency only if it's real, not manufactured)
6. CTA — sell the conversation, not the meeting ("Open to learning how?" beats "Do you have 15 minutes?")

## The actionable-content formula (applies to email bodies too)

Same rule as blog/social content: an insight without a next step reads as trivia, and a next step without the reasoning reads as a hard sell. Pair the two — state what's true, then exactly what to do about it — in the body of any value-driven newsletter or nurture email. (Full framing lives in `apptweak-writing-guidelines`'s hook library if this is AppTweak-branded content.)

## Newsletter / product-update structure

The goal for any newsletter, product release, or product update email: turn the topic into value for the reader even if they never click. If the email is only worth something after the CTA, it wasn't worth sending.

1. **Choose the topic your audience most cares about** — not what's easiest to write about this week.
2. **Create the value directly in the body** — a story (initial state → problem → transformation), a list of tips, or a problem breakdown. This is the actual payoff, not a teaser for it.
3. **Connect the dots** — "that's why we're doing this" / "that's why we built this," bridging the value just delivered to what you're about to offer.
4. **Share your message** — proof, credibility, or a specific detail that builds authority for the offer.
5. **CTA/CTV, leading with the outcome** — name what the reader gets, not the act of clicking.

## CTA discipline

Same three C's as other channels — correct (match the ask to where this relationship actually is; don't default to "book a demo" on a first cold touch), clear (one action), compelling (name the outcome, not the click). See `apptweak-product-release-update-template` for the fuller writeup if useful context.

## 10 things worth A/B testing on your own list

Reed's own reported results from testing across several B2B audiences — sender name, send time,
subject line phrasing and case, line breaks vs. block text, link count, story vs. direct teaching,
copy length, greeting, and HTML vs. plain text. Treat each as a hypothesis to test on your own
list, not a guarantee. Full list with results: `references/research-and-ab-tests.md`.

## Quick self-check before sending

- Does the subject line resolve into something real in the body, or does it overpromise?
- Is the first line about the recipient, or about you/your company?
- Would this survive being read on a phone in two scrolls or less?
- Is there one clear, correctly-staged next step — not a menu of asks?
- If this is AppTweak-branded: does it also clear the banned-words, ASO-positioning, and "We" pronoun guardrails in `apptweak-writing-guidelines`?
