# LinkedIn Post Craft

Structural and engagement tactics for LinkedIn specifically, paraphrased from Devin Reed's *Content That Converts*. This is the mechanics/structure layer — how a post is built so people actually read and engage with it, not how it sounds. It doesn't own voice for either personal or branded posts; it stacks with the skills that do:

**Reference material:** `references/playbook.md` in this skill's folder holds deeper/situational
material — five prompts for when you don't know what to post, the milestone/journey post shape
with a full worked example, and how to turn a good run of posts into a durable lead asset. Pull
from it when the situation calls for it; it isn't needed for a standard post.

## The hook (first 1-3 lines, before "See more")

LinkedIn truncates a post after roughly three lines, so the opener has one job: earn the click to expand. Two proven shapes:

- **Build anticipation.** Open mid-thought and cut the visible text right before the payoff, so "See more" is the only way to find out what you were about to say.
- **Build a mystery.** Pose something that raises a question the reader wants answered ("Then wait...") — same mechanic as a curiosity-led email subject line.

Supporting habits that make any hook land better:
- **Short sentences, one idea each.** Easy-to-read sentences are what let people keep reading; density (visually or cognitively) makes a post feel like work before they've even started.
- **Use pronouns.** "You," "I," "they," "we" make a post read as a conversation rather than a broadcast — small swap, consistently more relatable.
- **Don't over-explain the setup.** Get to the tension or the specific claim fast; the hook's job is curiosity, not context.
- **Leave the reader with an unspoken question.** Whether the hook is phrased as a statement or a literal question, its real job is to plant a question in the reader's head that only the rest of the post answers. Test it by finishing the sentence "...which makes the reader silently ask ___?" — if nothing fills that blank, the hook is stating a fact instead of opening a gap.
  - Example: "The best salespeople know more than process and tactics." → the reader's implicit question: *what else do they know?* The rest of the post exists to answer it.
  - This applies whether the hook is a statement ("The best salespeople know more than process and tactics") or a direct question ("Do you actually know how to use AI in your ASO analysis?") — the form doesn't matter, the gap it opens does.

## How LinkedIn actually scores a post (and what to do about it)

Three signals drive distribution: **interactions** (likes, comments, tags, shares), **"See more" click-throughs**, and **dwell time** (how long people actually stay on the post). Practical implications:

- **Reply to your own comments.** This keeps the conversation active, which the algorithm reads as ongoing value and rewards with further distribution.
- **Put links in the comments, not the post body.** LinkedIn suppresses reach on posts with outbound links because it wants readers to stay on-platform — move the link to a comment instead.
- **Never edit a published post**, even to fix a typo. Editing after publication appears to suppress reach (LinkedIn's likely reasoning: it doesn't want people changing the meaning of something already liked/shared). A typo is not worth the reach hit.
- **Don't chase the algorithm as a strategy.** It changes without notice and isn't publicly documented — the durable move is consistently useful content for people, which the algorithm rewards as a side effect, not a target to reverse-engineer.

## Visuals: video or a strong photo, almost always

A post with a visual reliably beats a text-only post. Confirmed by a real result: a post with a
video that gave the value upfront outperformed benchmark.

- **Video is the strongest option**, short — around 30 seconds, not longer. Give the value
  immediately in the first few seconds; don't build up to it the way you would in text.
- **If not video, use a photo that grabs attention** — color and/or a human face perform best.
  A flat product screenshot or a stock-looking image is the weak version of this; a real photo
  with a face or strong color is the strong version.
- **Treat "no visual" as the exception, not the default.** Before publishing, the default question
  is "what's the video or image for this post?" not "does this post need one?"

## Best time to post

Post between 9am–12pm, 12pm–3pm, or 3pm–6pm.

Best days, in order: **Monday, then Wednesday, then Tuesday.**

## Two post shapes, and picking one before drafting

Rather than reaching for one default structure every time, decide which shape the post actually
is first:

- **Short and controversial.** A single sentence (or close to it) that sparks an emotional
  reaction, often via a personal detail stated flatly and without hedging. Example shape: "27
  tattoos later and I still haven't 'ruined my chances at getting a good job.'" No build-up, no
  resolution needed beyond the line itself — the sentence *is* the post.
- **Longer and aspirational.** A first-person story that's clearly relatable to the audience, with
  a visible before/aha-moment/after arc, written so the truncation point (right before "See more")
  falls exactly where curiosity peaks. This is the shape the structure below and the hook guidance
  above are built for.

Most posts are the second shape; the first is a deliberate, occasional change of pace — don't
default to it just because it's shorter to write.

## A reliable post structure (from a specific viral-post breakdown)

Not the only shape that works, but a dependable default when starting from a blank page:

1. **Open with a familiar, relatable phrase or a bold claim** that the audience already recognizes as true.
2. **Tease the conflict before the cutoff** — hint that there's a "but" coming, without resolving it yet.
3. **Describe the old/broken way, in three beats** (rule of three — see below).
4. **State the negative consequence, also in three beats.**
5. **Contrast with what should happen instead** — the actual point of view.
6. **Tie back to the opening line** so the post feels closed, not just ended.

## Two closing moves worth adding deliberately

- **Summarize in one line** before the close — a single sentence that restates the point, so a
  reader who skimmed the middle still walks away with the takeaway.
- **A distinct finishing line**, separate from the summary line and separate from the CTA/CTV —
  gives the post a sense of landing rather than just stopping. Doesn't have to be clever, just
  present.

## Make it easy to skim — non-negotiable, not a nice-to-have

Write as if the reader's attention span is very short and they will not read closely — because
that's the reality on LinkedIn. Don't write for a smart, patient reader; write for someone
scrolling fast who will bounce at the first sign of effort. Concretely:

A LinkedIn post is read by scanning, not by committed reading, even more than a blog post is.
Build this in while drafting:
- **Leave visual space** between sections/beats — a wall of unbroken lines reads as work before
  anyone's read a word. When in doubt, add another line break.
- **Simple, everyday words** over anything that sounds like a report. If a word needs a
  dictionary, cut it.
- **Short sentences**, consistently, not just in the hook. One idea per sentence.
- **Lean on pronouns** ("I," "you," "we," "yours") — see the hook section above; it also reads as
  more conversational and less broadcast-y throughout the body, not just the opener.

## The rule of three

When listing examples, symptoms, or consequences inside a post, three items consistently reads better than two (feels thin) or four-plus (feels padded). Worth a specific check when editing a bulleted list inside a post.

## A clear point of view beats a neutral recap

A post that takes an actual stance — even a mildly contrarian one — travels further and builds a more qualified following than one that objectively summarizes a topic. People follow people who stand for something. This applies to profile headlines too: a specific point of view in a headline (rather than just a job title) gives people a reason to click through and follow.

## One problem per post

Solve exactly one problem for the audience per post — resist folding in a second tip or a related
side point just because it's relevant. A post that tries to cover two problems usually serves
neither well and is harder to skim (see above); save the second point for its own post.

## Relatable over expert

Worry less about establishing expertise and more about being relatable — a post that makes the
reader think "that's me" earns more trust than one that positions the writer as the authority in
the room. This is the same instinct behind `blog-writing-methodology`'s "content diary" approach,
applied at the single-post level: point at a real, recognizable experience rather than asserting
credentials.

## "Simple sells" and "examples sell"

- **Conversational language over jargon.** "Use" beats "utilize." Writing for a collegiate reading level doesn't read as smart, it reads as harder to relate to.
- **Short sentences, mixed with medium ones** so the rhythm doesn't feel choppy.
- **One specific example beats a superlative claim.** "Best," "largest," "smartest" persuade less than a single concrete scenario the reader recognizes as their own problem.

## Mindset notes (useful when a post underperforms or gets pushback)

- **Some disagreement is a sign a post had a real point of view, not a failure.** Ignore trolling rather than engaging it.
- **Engagement is naturally uneven week to week**, even for experienced creators — look for patterns across several posts rather than reacting to any single one.

## Quick self-check before posting

- Does the first visible line (before "See more") earn the click on its own?
- Can I finish "...which makes the reader silently ask ___?" for the hook — or does it just state a fact with nothing left open?
- Is there an actual point of view here, or just a summary?
- If there's a list inside the post, is it three items, not two or five?
- Is any link moved to the comments rather than the post body?
- Did I pick a shape (short-controversial vs. longer-aspirational) on purpose, rather than
  defaulting to whichever is faster to write?
- Would someone skimming — not reading closely — still get the point? Enough space, simple
  words, short sentences throughout, not just in the hook?
- Is there a one-line summary and a distinct finishing line before any CTA?
- Does this post solve exactly one problem, not two folded together?
- Is this reading as relatable, or as a résumé?
- If it's a milestone/journey post, does reader value (the story, the insight) land before the
  passive CTA, not after? (Full five-beat shape and a worked example: `references/playbook.md`.)
- Stuck for a topic? `references/playbook.md` has five reliable prompts.
- If AppTweak-branded: does it also clear `apptweak-writing-guidelines`'s banned-words, ASO-positioning, and "We" pronoun checks?
- Is there a visual? A short video (~30s, value upfront) or an attention-grabbing photo (color/a
  face) beats no visual almost every time.
- Is this scheduled for Mon/Tue/Wed, between 9am-6pm?
