# Messaging & Positioning Framework Generator

This skill produces layered messaging architectures — not taglines in isolation. You will build a complete system from strategic narrative down to persona-specific talk tracks, stress-tested for differentiation and grounded in competitive reality. Every output connects positioning choices to buyer evidence.

## Workflow

### Phase 1: Discovery & Inputs

Gather the raw material. The quality of your messaging is capped by the quality of your inputs.

**Minimum viable inputs (you need at least these):**
- What the product does (features, capabilities)
- Who buys it (title, company size, industry)
- What problem it solves
- 2-3 named competitors or alternatives

**Ideal inputs (ask for all of these):**
- Customer interview quotes or win/loss data
- Current positioning or messaging (even if outdated)
- Product demo or documentation access
- Sales objections heard frequently
- Pricing model and tier structure
- Recent wins and why customers chose them
- Company stage and growth goals

**Decision tree for how to proceed:**

If the user provides customer interviews or win/loss data:
- Extract the language buyers actually use. Mirror it. This is your highest-signal input.
- Identify the "before state" and "after state" from their words.
- Note which alternatives they evaluated and why they switched.

If the user provides only a website and product description:
- Audit the current website messaging first. Identify what is claimed vs. what is proven.
- Flag undifferentiated claims ("easy to use", "powerful", "all-in-one").
- Infer the buyer from the product — who has this pain badly enough to pay?

If the user provides competitor information:
- Map the competitive landscape before writing a single word of positioning.
- Identify what competitors concede — the gaps they leave open.

**Before moving to Phase 2, confirm with the user:**
1. The primary buyer persona (one — not three)
2. The top 3-5 competitive alternatives (including status quo)
3. The single most important outcome the buyer wants

### Phase 2: Competitive Landscape Mapping

Do not skip this phase. Positioning is relative — it only exists in contrast to alternatives.

**Map ALL alternatives, not just logo competitors:**
1. **Direct competitors** — same category, similar product
2. **Adjacent competitors** — different category, overlapping use case
3. **Status quo** — spreadsheets, manual processes, "we just live with it"
4. **Build in-house** — engineering team builds a custom solution
5. **Do nothing** — the pain is not severe enough to act

For each alternative, document:
- What it does well (be honest — stolen credibility hurts you)
- What it does poorly or ignores
- What buyers must believe to choose it
- Its implicit positioning (even if unstated)

**Find the gap.** Look for the intersection of:
- Something buyers care about deeply
- Something your product does demonstrably well
- Something no alternative addresses adequately

This gap is your positioning territory. If no gap exists, the product has a positioning problem that messaging cannot solve — flag this to the user.

**Quality gate:** You must be able to complete this sentence before proceeding: "For buyers who need [specific outcome], the current alternatives fail because [specific gap], and we address this by [specific capability]."

### Phase 3: Positioning Development

Read **[references/positioning-worksheet.md](references/positioning-worksheet.md)** now. Work through it step by step. Do not freelance the positioning — follow the worksheet.

Execute the Obviously Awesome framework in this exact order:

**Step 1: List competitive alternatives** (pull from Phase 2)
Write them down. Include status quo. Include "do nothing."

**Step 2: List unique attributes**
What can you do that alternatives cannot? Be ruthlessly specific. "Better UX" is not an attribute. "Completes onboarding setup in 3 clicks vs. 15+ configuration steps" is an attribute.

**Step 3: Translate attributes to value**
For each unique attribute, answer: "So what? What does this let the buyer DO or AVOID?" This is the value. Attributes are features. Value is outcome.

**Step 4: Identify who cares the most**
Which buyer segment has this pain the worst? Who gets fired if this problem is not solved? Who has budget and urgency? Narrow aggressively. "Mid-market SaaS companies" is too broad. "Series B-C SaaS companies losing customers in the first 90 days" is a segment.

**Step 5: Pick a market category**
This is the context that makes your value obvious. Choose the frame of reference that makes your strengths matter most. See the worksheet for the decision framework on category selection.

**Step 6: Write the positioning statement**
Use this structure:
"For [target segment] who [situation/need], [product] is the [market category] that [key differentiator] unlike [primary alternative] which [limitation]."

Write three versions. Pick the sharpest one. Sharp means specific, not clever.

**Quality gate:** Read the positioning statement aloud. If it could describe two different products, it is not specific enough. Rewrite until it passes.

### Phase 4: Messaging Hierarchy Construction

Build four layers. Each layer serves a different context and audience. Work top-down — the strategic narrative governs everything below it.

**Layer 1: Strategic Narrative (the "why now" story)**
- 150-250 words. Written as a narrative arc, not bullet points.
- Structure: Old world (how things were) → Shift (what changed) → New world (what is now possible) → Your role (how you enable the new world)
- This is NOT about your product. It is about the market shift that makes your product inevitable.
- The buyer should nod along for the first 80% before you mention your product.

**Layer 2: Core Positioning (the identity)**
- Tagline: 5-8 words. Captures the singular promise. No punctuation tricks.
- One-liner: 15-25 words. Expands the tagline into a complete value statement.
- Elevator pitch: 50-75 words. Includes problem, solution, differentiation, and outcome.
- Boilerplate: 75-100 words. The "about us" paragraph for press releases and bios.

**Layer 3: Key Message Pillars (the proof structure)**
- Exactly 3-4 pillars. Not 2 (too thin). Not 5+ (too fragmented for recall).
- Each pillar has: a headline claim, a supporting explanation (2-3 sentences), and 2-3 proof points (metrics, customer quotes, analyst validation).
- Pillars must be MECE — mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive of your value.
- Test: can a sales rep remember all pillars without notes? If not, simplify.

**Layer 4: Persona Variants**
- For each priority persona, create a messaging card with:
  - Their core pain (in their language, not yours)
  - The promise that resonates with their role
  - The proof points most relevant to their KPIs
  - One objection pre-handle
  - The CTA appropriate to their buying stage
- Limit to 3 personas maximum. More than 3 means you have not prioritized.

**Quality gate:** Read all four layers in sequence. They must tell a coherent story from top to bottom. If Layer 3 introduces ideas not grounded in Layer 1, something is misaligned. Fix it before proceeding.

### Phase 5: Differentiation Stress-Test

Apply the 4-question test to EVERY differentiator and claim in the messaging hierarchy. This is where weak messaging dies. Let it.

For each differentiator, ask:

1. **Is it TRUE?** Can you prove this with evidence the buyer would accept? Customer quotes, third-party data, product demos — not internal assertions.
2. **Is it RELEVANT?** Does the target buyer actually care about this? Would they pay more or switch vendors for it? If it is true but irrelevant, cut it.
3. **Is it UNIQUE?** Can competitors honestly make the same claim? If two competitors can say the same thing, it is table stakes, not differentiation. Demote it to a "requirements met" footnote.
4. **Is it SUSTAINABLE?** Will this still be true in 12-18 months? If a competitor can replicate it in one quarter, it is a temporary advantage, not a positioning foundation.

**Scoring:** A differentiator must pass all four tests to anchor a key message pillar. If it passes three, it can be a supporting proof point. If it passes two or fewer, remove it from the messaging entirely.

**Be honest.** The most common failure mode is claiming differentiation that is merely a feature competitors also have. Press hard. If you are uncertain whether something is truly unique, it probably is not.

### Phase 6: Documentation & Validation Plan

Read **[references/example-messaging-doc.md](references/example-messaging-doc.md)** before writing the final document. Match its structure, depth, and specificity.

Package the output in the requested format (see Output Formats below). Then read **[references/validation-playbook.md](references/validation-playbook.md)** and recommend a validation plan appropriate to the user's stage and resources.

Always include:
- An "Approved Language" section — exact phrases to use
- A "Forbidden Language" section — phrases that are banned and why
- A version number and review date
- Recommended next steps for testing

**Quality gate:** Before delivering, verify every claim in the document has at least one proof point. Flag any claim that is asserted without evidence and mark it "[NEEDS PROOF]".

## Messaging Hierarchy Structure

| Layer | Purpose | Length | Tone | Evidence Required |
|-------|---------|--------|------|-------------------|
| Strategic Narrative | Set market context, create urgency | 150-250 words | Visionary, confident, buyer-empathetic | Market data, trend references |
| Core Positioning | Define identity and singular promise | Tagline (5-8 words), One-liner (15-25 words), Pitch (50-75 words), Boilerplate (75-100 words) | Clear, specific, jargon-free | Differentiator must be provable |
| Key Message Pillars | Prove the promise with structured claims | 3-4 pillars, each with headline + 2-3 sentences + 2-3 proof points | Assertive, evidence-backed | Customer metrics, quotes, third-party validation |
| Persona Variants | Adapt messages to buyer-specific pain and language | One card per persona (pain, promise, proof, objection handle, CTA) | Role-specific, empathetic, action-oriented | Role-relevant KPIs and outcomes |

## Differentiation Quality Test

Apply this to every differentiator before it earns a spot in the messaging:

| Question | What "pass" looks like | What "fail" looks like |
|----------|----------------------|----------------------|
| **TRUE?** | You can cite a customer quote, metric, or demo-able capability. A skeptical buyer would accept the evidence. | It is an aspiration, a roadmap item, or an internal belief without external proof. |
| **RELEVANT?** | Target buyers mention this pain unprompted. It appears in win/loss data. Buyers would pay a premium for it. | It is a technical capability that impresses engineers but does not change a buyer's decision. |
| **UNIQUE?** | No more than one competitor can credibly make the same claim. You checked their websites and sales decks. | Three competitors say the same thing on their homepage. It is table stakes dressed as differentiation. |
| **SUSTAINABLE?** | The advantage is rooted in architecture, data, or network effects — not a feature that can be copied in a sprint. | A well-funded competitor could replicate this in one quarter with a small team. |

## Output Formats

### Full Messaging Document
Use when building messaging from scratch or doing a major overhaul. Includes all four hierarchy layers, differentiation analysis, approved/forbidden language, persona cards, and validation plan. Typically 300-500 lines. Read the example document before writing this.

### Positioning One-Pager
Use for executive alignment or board communication. Includes: positioning statement, 3-4 key differentiators, target segment definition, competitive frame, and the strategic narrative condensed to one paragraph. Fits on one page.

### Persona Messaging Cards
Use for enabling sales teams quickly. One card per persona. Includes: pain statement, promise, 3 proof points, top objection with response, and recommended CTA. Each card fits on a half-page.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why It Fails | What To Do Instead |
|---------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Feature-first messaging** | Buyers do not care what it does until they understand why it matters. Features are proof, not the headline. | Lead with the outcome the buyer gets. Use features only as evidence under message pillars. |
| **Undifferentiated claims** | "Easy to use", "powerful", "all-in-one", "seamless" — every competitor says these. They are noise. | Replace with specific, provable claims. "Reduces onboarding time from 14 days to 3" beats "fast onboarding." |
| **Too many messages** | More than 4 pillars fractures recall. Sales cannot remember them. Buyers cannot either. | Force-rank to 3-4 pillars. Everything else is supporting detail, not a headline. |
| **Positioning by negation** | "We are not like X" tells the buyer what you are against, not what you are for. It also elevates the competitor. | Position FOR something. State what you enable, not what you oppose. |
| **Insider jargon** | Internal shorthand and technical terms that mean nothing to buyers. "Our platform leverages proprietary AI orchestration." | Use the words buyers use. If it does not appear in customer interviews, it does not appear in messaging. |
| **Ignoring the status quo** | Treating named competitors as the only alternative. Most deals are lost to "do nothing" or spreadsheets. | Always include status quo and manual processes in your competitive landscape. Position against inaction. |
| **Skipping persona adaptation** | One message for all buyers. The CRO cares about revenue; the VP CS cares about retention. Same product, different story. | Build persona cards with role-specific pain, proof, and CTAs. Reuse pillars but reframe for each audience. |
| **Aspirational positioning** | Positioning based on the roadmap, not the current product. Messaging promises what does not yet exist. | Position on what is true today. Flag roadmap items as future proof points and exclude from current messaging. |
